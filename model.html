StageAgesRelational layerFinancial-progression layer
Early years0–5Parents are usually in their late twenties to thirties, and grandparents often help with care and gifts.Child care and parental leave, a first savings account, and family income shaping most options.
School years6–11Parents are mid-career and grandparents may be nearing retirement.School costs, clubs and hobbies, pocket money and the first lessons about saving.
Dependent years12–17Parents are usually mid-career, so household income shapes what support you can reach.First tax file number, part-time work, school costs. Super starts with your first employer contributions.
Leaving home18–23Parents income can still count for student payments. Grandparents may start needing support.Youth Allowance or ABSTUDY, HELP debt, first full-time pay, low-income super offsets.
Establishing24–29Independence tests are mostly met. Parents are in their fifties.Rent or buy, first home concessions, keeping track of super across jobs.
Building30–37A partner and children bring first wills and guardianship questions. Parents approach retirement.Mortgage, family payments, salary sacrifice, insurance inside super.
Peak load38–47Sandwich years: children costs and parents ageing needs overlap.Higher earnings, investment property choices, super catch-ups, wills and powers of attorney become urgent.
Pre-retirement48–57Parents often face aged care decisions while your own estate plan matures. Adult children may leave or return.Contribution caps, trust structures, transition planning, gifting questions.
Transition58–63Inheritances may arrive and you may be caring.Accessing super, transition to retirement, Age Pension tests ahead.
Drawdown64–75You may be the one navigating aged care, with adult children and grandchildren in your plan.Pension phase, Age Pension tests, downsizing, estate instruments.
Later life76–85Family help and communication become central, and executors and families may need practical guidance.Aged care costs, village or home care choices, settling the estate, gifts and legacy.

Income awareness without asking about income

Four emphasis weights (safety net, building, established, structuring) start from an age prior. Opened topics add to them, and the behavioural share grows with use but is capped. A floor keeps every weight above zero, and behaviour decays whenever time moves on, so no one is locked into a bracket. All eight areas appear in every column. Only their order moves. The three income scenarios on the map (lower, middle, higher) are highlighted by the same weights: lower leans on safety net plus half of building, middle on half of building and half of established, higher on half of established plus structuring. The age priors are design assumptions and are listed in the register for calibration.

Household isolation (a possible future version)

  1. Household

    The only tenant. One sign-in, one private space, one set of policies.

  2. People

    You, plus any parents, grandparents, partner, children and grandchildren you add. Each has an age band, a label and a living arrangement, nothing more.

  3. Interactions

    Which topics were opened and when. Used for branch memory and the emphasis.

  4. Derived emphasis

    Four weights per person, recomputed from age and interactions, decaying over time.

Rules

  • Every row carries a household identifier. Database policies refuse any read or write where it does not match the signed-in household.
  • Parents, grandparents, partner, children and grandchildren are records you enter in your own household. They are not links to other people's accounts.
  • If a parent also uses the planner, connecting needs an invitation they accept. They choose which fields to share, and revoking removes the shared copy.
  • Caching, search and analytics are keyed by household. No aggregate is published below a minimum group size.
  • Export and delete cover the whole household, including the derived emphasis.

Never collected

  • Income or a bracket as a typed field. The weights are derived and can be reset.
  • Tax file numbers, myGov or Centrelink logins, or fund member numbers.
  • Health details. The aged care topics use only the age band the user chose.
  • Names of family members beyond a label the user picks, such as Mum.

What is real and what is not yet

  • Working: the family map, linked topics that appear and disappear with age bands, emphasis that shifts with age and use, branch memory, adjacent paths, the quick and guided paths, and state-aware support lists.
  • Specified only: the household tables, the isolation policies, invitations between related users, and the figure registry that would block stale numbers from showing.
  • Carried over from coeconomics.com, the original planner: considerations, income scenarios and group figures for bands 12 to 81. The 64 to 65 considerations could not be read when copied. Ages 0 to 11 and 82 to 99 have no pages there, so the map shows planner drafts marked as such. None of it has been checked against primary sources.

This version: saved in your browser only

The published map has no backend and no account. The household you build (role labels, age bands, living arrangements, chosen state, quick or guided path, and the selected person) is stored in this browser under the key familymap.au.household.v1, on this device only. Nothing is sent to a server, and the site makes no network requests beyond its own files. The site's security policy blocks any other connection.

Use “Reset / clear saved household” on the map, or clear this site's data in your browser settings, to remove it. Anyone using the same browser profile can see it. Use role labels such as Mum or Child 1, not real names.

Privacy

Your household (role labels, age bands, living arrangements, state, path and selected person) is saved only in this browser, under the name “familymap.au.household.v1”. Nothing is sent to a server. There is no account, no cookies and no analytics.
